When does it rain in Tokyo?

39 years of daily rain, three ways to model it, and one day of the year that is almost never wet.

2024 · 4 min read

A classic dataset: for every calendar day from 1951 to 1989, how many years did Tokyo get more than 1 mm of rain on that day? That gives 366 numbers \(y_t\) out of \(n_t\) years each (39 for most days, but only 10 for 29 February). Together with Baptiste Tesson, I used it to compare three very different ways of fitting the same Bayesian model.

The data

  • June is the wettest stretch: tsuyu, the East Asian rainy season. On 26 June, it rained in 23 of the 39 years.
  • There's a second bump in September, typhoon and autumn-rain season.
  • Winter is dry. In December and January it rains on only about 15% of days.
  • 29 February had zero rainy days. But it only exists in leap years, so that's 0 out of 10, not 0 out of 39. Always check your denominators.

From day to day the raw data is very spiky. The goal is the smooth seasonal probability underneath.

The model

Each day gets a latent value \(x_t\) on the logit scale, and the observations are binomial:

$$y_t \mid x_t \sim \operatorname{Bin}\big(n_t, \pi(x_t)\big), \qquad \pi(x_t) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-x_t}}.$$

Smoothness comes from a first-order random walk, which says neighbouring days should be similar:

$$x_t = x_{t-1} + u_t, \qquad u_t \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma_u^2),$$

with an inverse gamma prior \(\sigma_u^2 \sim \operatorname{IG}(\alpha, \beta)\). The single hyperparameter \(\sigma_u^2\) controls everything: small means a stiff curve, large lets it follow every spike.

Try: drag σᵤ and press redraw. Notice: these are random seasons drawn from the prior alone, before seeing any data. σᵤ decides how wiggly a season is allowed to be.

MCMC

Gibbs step for the variance

Because the inverse gamma prior is conjugate to the random walk, the full conditional of \(\sigma_u^2\) is again inverse gamma, with updated parameters:

$$\sigma_u^2 \mid \mathbf x \sim \operatorname{IG}\Big(\alpha + \tfrac{T-1}{2},\;\; \beta + \tfrac12 \textstyle\sum_{t=2}^{T} (x_t - x_{t-1})^2\Big).$$

Metropolis–Hastings for the curve

For the \(x_t\) we used Metropolis–Hastings, proposing new values from the random-walk prior itself, conditional on the neighbours. That choice makes the prior terms cancel in the acceptance ratio, which reduces to a likelihood ratio:

$$\alpha_{\text{acc}} = \min\left(1,\; \exp\sum_{i \in I}\Big[\,y_i\,(x_i' - x_i) + n_i \log\frac{1 + e^{x_i}}{1 + e^{x_i'}}\Big]\right).$$

Conditional proposals for a block \(I\) come straight from the Gaussian structure of the random walk. With precision matrix \(\mathbf Q\) partitioned into \(I\) and the rest,

$$\mathbf x_I \mid \mathbf x_{-I} \sim \mathcal N\big(-\mathbf Q_{II}^{-1}\mathbf Q_{I,-I}\,\mathbf x_{-I},\; \mathbf Q_{II}^{-1}\big),$$

sampled efficiently through a Cholesky factorisation.

One day at a time, or in blocks?

We ran it both ways: updating one day at a time, and updating blocks of \(M\) days at once. After scanning block sizes, \(M = 10\) gave the best balance between acceptance rate and runtime.

SamplerAcceptance rateRuntime
Single-site MH0.916181 s
Block MH, \(M = 10\)0.458465 s

A very high acceptance rate isn't automatically good; it can mean the chain only takes small steps. Blocks propose bigger moves that are rejected more often. Both chains' trace plots looked like white noise, and the posterior histograms were essentially the same, so here the extra cost of blocking didn't buy much.

INLA

INLA skips sampling entirely and approximates the posterior with nested Laplace approximations. It's designed for exactly this kind of latent Gaussian model. With a log-gamma prior on the log precision (the equivalent of our inverse gamma), the results were practically identical to MCMC.

181 ssingle-site MCMC
465 sblock MCMC
≈ 4 sINLA, same answer

We also compared INLA's settings: the full Laplace approximation against the default simplified one, and a full grid against the central composite design for integrating over the hyperparameter. The results were identical and the runtimes similar. With only one hyperparameter, there's little to gain from the more expensive settings.

Optimisation

Finally we tried a Laplace approach with RTMB. We couldn't get it to cooperate, so we fell back on maximising with R's built-in optim. The result captured the overall seasonal shape (wet summer, dry winter) but smoothed over the smaller wiggles that MCMC and INLA picked up.

MCMC and INLA gave practically the same posterior. INLA did it in about 4 seconds instead of several minutes, which matters more than it sounds when you want to try many variants of a model.
