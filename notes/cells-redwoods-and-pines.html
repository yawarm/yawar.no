Cells, redwoods and pine trees

Three sets of dots that look almost the same but come from three different spatial processes.

2024 · 4 min read

Three datasets that, at first glance, are just dots in a square: biological cells under a microscope, young redwood trees, and pine trees in a forest. Here they are, drawn from the actual data:

They look similar, but they come from different kinds of spatial processes:

  • Cells keep their distance. Each cell takes up space, so neighbours can't sit on top of each other. That's repulsion: more regular than randomness.
  • Redwoods stick together. Young redwoods often sprout from the roots of a parent tree, and roots only reach so far, so you get little families. That's clustering.
  • Pines look random. Seeds blown by the wind or carried by birds and squirrels end up more or less anywhere.

The question is how to say that with maths instead of squinting.

The baseline: complete spatial randomness

The reference model is the homogeneous Poisson point process with intensity \(\lambda\). For any region \(B\) of area \(|B|\), the number of points is Poisson,

$$N(B) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda |B|),$$

and counts in disjoint regions are independent. No attraction, no repulsion: every point ignores every other point.

Ripley's K and the L-function

Ripley's \(K\)-function asks: around a typical point, how many other points are within distance \(r\), relative to the intensity? Its estimator (ignoring edge corrections) is

$$\hat K(r) = \frac{|W|}{n(n-1)} \sum_{i \ne j} \mathbf 1\big[\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert \le r\big],$$

where \(|W|\) is the area of the window. For a Poisson process, \(K(r) = \pi r^2\). It's easier to read after a transformation that turns it into a straight line,

$$L(r) = \sqrt{K(r)/\pi}, \qquad L(r) = r \text{ under complete randomness.}$$

So plot \(L(r)\) against \(r\): above the line means more neighbours than chance (clustering), below means fewer (repulsion).

Try: move over the cells and drag the radius. Notice: the circle counts neighbours within r. Averaged over every point, that count is what Ripley's K measures, compared with what pure randomness would give.

Testing against randomness

An empirical L-function always wiggles, so the question is whether it wiggles more than randomness would. I simulated 100 Poisson patterns with the same number of points, computed their L-functions, and used them to build a 90% band. The results:

Redwoodsabove the band at small \(r\)clustering: model with a Neyman–Scott process
Pinesinside the band for all \(r\)Poisson is reasonable

Fitting a Strauss process

For the cells I fitted a Strauss process. Its density penalises every pair of points closer than an interaction radius \(r_0\):

$$f(\mathbf x) \propto \beta^{\,n(\mathbf x)}\,\gamma^{\,s(\mathbf x)}, \qquad s(\mathbf x) = \#\{(i,j) : \lVert x_i - x_j\rVert \lt r_0\},\quad 0 \le \gamma \le 1.$$

With \(\gamma = 1\) it's Poisson; with \(\gamma = 0\) close pairs are forbidden outright (hard core). Reading the empirical L-function, points avoid coming closer than about 0.1, so I set \(r_0 = 0.1\) and an intensity of about 40 points per unit area. Simulations from that model produced L-functions that lined up well with the real cells.

Trees that weren't detected

A second part of the project was about a forest of 300 m × 300 m, split into a 30 × 30 grid of 10 m × 10 m cells. In each cell, a survey had detected \(M_{ij}\) pines, but every tree was only spotted with probability \(\alpha_{ij}\). If the true count is \(N_{ij}\), then

$$N_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(100\lambda), \qquad M_{ij} \mid N_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N_{ij}, \alpha_{ij}).$$

Correcting for the detection probability gives a simple estimator of the intensity,

$$\hat\lambda = \frac{1}{|W|\,\bar\alpha}\sum_{i,j} M_{ij} = 0.0104 \text{ trees per m}^2,$$

or about 104 pines per hectare. Bayes' theorem then gives the posterior for the true count in each cell,

$$P(N_{ij} = n \mid M_{ij} = m) \propto \binom{n}{m}\alpha_{ij}^m(1-\alpha_{ij})^{n-m}\,\frac{e^{-100\lambda}(100\lambda)^n}{n!},\quad n \ge m,$$

which shifts expected counts upwards wherever detection was poor, and makes the uncertainty uneven across the forest: small where the survey was reliable, large where it wasn't.

The three pictures look almost the same, but the L-function separates them clearly into repulsion, clustering and randomness.
