Clustered, random or evenly spread?

Three sets of dots that look almost the same but come from three different spatial processes.

2024 · 4 min read

We will consider three point pattern datasets, which at first look like dots in a square. The first is biological cells seen in a microscope, the second is young redwood trees, and the third is pine trees in a forest. Here they are, drawn from the data:

The patterns look similar, but they come from different processes:

  • The cells are evenly spread out and show repulsion. Each cell takes up space, so the cells cannot sit on top of each other, and the space between them is maximised.
  • The redwoods are clustered, which makes sense since young redwoods often grow from the roots of a parent tree, and the roots only reach so far.
  • The pines seem to be randomly scattered. Pine seeds are spread by the wind, and pine nuts are carried around by birds and squirrels, which is close to random.

To say this more precisely than by looking at the plots, we need some tools from spatial statistics.

Complete spatial randomness

The reference model is the homogeneous Poisson point process with intensity \(\lambda\). For any region \(B\) with area \(|B|\), the number of points in \(B\) is Poisson distributed,

$$N(B) \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda |B|),$$

and counts in disjoint regions are independent. The points do not attract or repel each other.

Ripley's K and the L-function

Ripley's \(K\)-function measures how many other points we find within a distance \(r\) of a typical point, relative to the intensity. If we ignore edge corrections, it can be estimated by

$$\begin{aligned}\hat K(r) &= \frac{|W|}{n(n-1)} \\ &\quad \times \sum_{i \ne j} \mathbf 1\big[\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert \le r\big],\end{aligned}$$

where \(|W|\) is the area of the observation window. For a Poisson process, \(K(r) = \pi r^2\). It is easier to read after transforming it to a straight line,

$$\begin{gathered}L(r) = \sqrt{K(r)/\pi}, \\ L(r) = r \text{ under complete randomness.}\end{gathered}$$

If the L-function lies above the line, there are more neighbours than we would expect by chance, which indicates clustering. If it lies below, there are fewer, which indicates repulsion.

Move over the cells and change the radius. The circle shows the neighbours within r. Ripley's K is based on this count averaged over all points, compared with what a random pattern would give.

Testing against randomness

An empirical L-function will never be exactly a straight line, so we need to know how much it would vary for a Poisson process. I simulated 100 Poisson patterns with the same number of points, computed their L-functions and used them to make a 90% prediction band. The results were:

DatasetEmpirical L vs. 90% bandConclusion
Cellsbelow the band for small \(r\)repulsion, model with a Strauss process
Redwoodsabove the band for small \(r\)clustering, model with a Neyman–Scott process
Pinesinside the band for all \(r\)a Poisson process is reasonable

Fitting a Strauss process

For the cells I used a Strauss process. Its density penalises every pair of points that are closer than an interaction distance \(r_0\):

$$\begin{gathered}f(\mathbf x) \propto \beta^{\,n(\mathbf x)}\,\gamma^{\,s(\mathbf x)}, \\ s(\mathbf x) = \#\{(i,j) : \lVert x_i - x_j\rVert \lt r_0\},\quad 0 \le \gamma \le 1.\end{gathered}$$

With \(\gamma = 1\) we get the Poisson process back, and with \(\gamma = 0\) close pairs are not allowed at all. From the empirical L-function, the cells seem to avoid coming closer than about 0.1, so I set \(r_0 = 0.1\) and an intensity of about 40 points per unit area. Simulations from this model gave L-functions that matched the cells quite well.

Trees that were not detected

A second part of the project was about a 300 m × 300 m forest, divided into a 30 × 30 grid of 10 m × 10 m cells. In each cell, a survey had detected \(M_{ij}\) pines, but each tree was only detected with probability \(\alpha_{ij}\). If the true number of trees is \(N_{ij}\), then

$$\begin{gathered}N_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(100\lambda), \\ M_{ij} \mid N_{ij} \sim \operatorname{Binomial}(N_{ij}, \alpha_{ij}).\end{gathered}$$

Correcting for the detection probability gives an estimator for the intensity,

$$\hat\lambda = \frac{1}{|W|\,\bar\alpha}\sum_{i,j} M_{ij} = 0.0104 \text{ trees per m}^2,$$

which is about 104 pines per hectare. Using Bayes' theorem, we get the distribution of the true count in each cell given what was observed,

$$\begin{aligned}P(N_{ij} = n \mid M_{ij} = m) &\propto \binom{n}{m}\alpha_{ij}^m(1-\alpha_{ij})^{n-m} \\ &\quad \times \frac{e^{-100\lambda}(100\lambda)^n}{n!}, \qquad n \ge m.\end{aligned}$$

The expected counts move up in cells where the detection probability was low. The standard deviations also vary a lot over the grid, and they are lower in cells with more observed trees, since the data tells us more about those cells.

So the three patterns look almost the same in a plot, but the L-function separates them clearly into repulsion, clustering and randomness.

Try it yourself

Pick a dataset. The left panel shows the points, and the right panel shows \(L(r) - r\), which is zero for complete randomness. The shaded band is the 90% range from 100 simulated Poisson patterns with the same number of points. Above the band indicates clustering, and below indicates repulsion.

When you click through the datasets, the cells go below the grey band (repulsion), the redwoods go above it (clustering) and the pines stay inside it.
