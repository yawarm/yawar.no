Teaching a camera 10,000 products

A self-checkout system that recognises products from camera images, now live in ten grocery stores. Recognition is a similarity search, not a fixed list of classes.

2025–26 · 6 min read

From 2025 to 2026 I worked on a self-checkout system for NorgesGruppen. Cameras watch the checkout area, and the system has to recognise every product a customer handles. It's now live in 10 stores, running on NVIDIA Jetson computers inside the stores themselves.

There's a lot going on in that system. It's built on NVIDIA DeepStream, objects are tracked from frame to frame with Kalman filter-based trackers that I tuned for store conditions, and it watches for items that never pass the scanner. This note is about the recognition part.

Why not a classifier?

The obvious approach is a classifier: one output per product, trained on labelled images. With more than 10,000 products that gets painful fast. New products arrive all the time, packaging changes, seasonal items come and go, and every change means retraining the last layer and shipping a new model to every store.

So instead of a fixed list of classes, the model maps every image to an embedding, a vector \(\mathbf z = g(\text{image}) \in \mathbb R^D\), and the aim is for images of the same product to end up close together. Recognition then becomes a search problem. With normalised embeddings, closeness is cosine similarity,

$$\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf z, \mathbf z_j) = \frac{\mathbf z^\top \mathbf z_j}{\lVert\mathbf z\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf z_j\rVert},$$

and a FAISS vector index finds the most similar known products among 10,000+ items quickly. Adding a new product means adding its embeddings to the index. Nothing has to be retrained.

Try: drag the two arrow tips. Notice: the similarity only cares about the angle between them, not their length. Pointing the same way gives 1, at right angles 0, opposite −1.

Voting, and unknown products

Given the \(k\) nearest neighbours \(\mathcal N_k(\mathbf z)\), the simplest decision rule is a vote:

$$\hat y = \arg\max_c \sum_{j \in \mathcal N_k(\mathbf z)} \mathbf 1[y_j = c].$$

But a vote always picks something. In a store, a confident wrong answer at the checkout is worse than asking for help, so the system also needs an "unknown" option, for example when even the nearest neighbour is too far away. This is the open-set problem, and it's the reason the demo at the bottom of this page has a threshold slider.

DINOv3

The embeddings come from DINOv3, Meta's self-supervised vision transformer. It was trained on an enormous number of images without any labels, by learning to give consistent representations to different views of the same image. The result is a model that already has a very good general sense of shape, texture and colour.

But "very good in general" isn't the same as "can tell two almost identical yogurts apart under shop lighting". It needed to specialise.

Fine-tuning with LoRA

Fine-tuning every weight in a large model is expensive, needs a lot of data, and risks making it forget what it already knew. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) freezes the original weight matrix \(W_0 \in \mathbb R^{d\times k}\) and learns a low-rank update next to it:

$$W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BA, \qquad B \in \mathbb R^{d\times r},\; A \in \mathbb R^{r\times k},\; r \ll \min(d,k).$$

\(B\) starts at zero, so training begins exactly at the pre-trained model and moves away from it only as far as the data asks. The savings are large. For one \(1024 \times 1024\) projection, a typical size in a large vision transformer:

$$\underbrace{1024^2 \approx 1.05\text{M}}_{\text{full fine-tuning}} \quad\text{vs.}\quad \underbrace{2 \cdot 8 \cdot 1024 \approx 16\text{k}}_{\text{LoRA, } r=8} \;\approx 1.6\%.$$

Try: drag the rank r. Notice: even at r = 64, LoRA trains a small fraction of what full fine-tuning would for one 1024 × 1024 layer.

In other words, you train a small correction to the model instead of rewriting it. Training is faster, it needs far less memory and data, and the general knowledge in the original model stays intact.

Rare products and store lighting

Stores are messy. Some products are bought so rarely that there are hardly any images of them, and checkout lighting is nothing like a photo studio. So the training data was augmented: flipped, stretched, geometrically distorted and varied, so that the model saw rare products and difficult lighting far more often than reality would offer. That improved classification accuracy.

What I learned

  • The problem formulation mattered most. Treating recognition as retrieval is what made 10,000+ products and a changing catalogue manageable.
  • A foundation model with a small adapter is a good default when there's little data per class.
  • The long tail was mostly a data problem. The rare products needed more varied training data more than a different model.
  • Running on the edge is a constraint from the start. When the model lives on a small box in a store, latency and memory shape every other decision.
