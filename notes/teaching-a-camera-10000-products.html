How does a checkout recognise 10,000 products?

A self-checkout system that recognises products from camera images, now running in ten grocery stores. The products are recognised with a similarity search over a database of embeddings.

2025–26 · 6 min read

From 2025 to 2026 I worked on a self-checkout system for NorgesGruppen. Cameras look at the checkout area, and the system has to recognise the products a customer handles. The system is now running in 10 stores, on NVIDIA Jetson computers placed in the stores.

The system is built on NVIDIA DeepStream. Objects are tracked from frame to frame with Kalman filter based trackers, which I tuned for the conditions in the stores, and the system also looks for items that do not pass the scanner. In this note I will mostly write about how the products are recognised.

Classifier or similarity search

The first idea would be a classifier with one output for each product, trained on labelled images. With more than 10,000 products this becomes hard to maintain. New products come in all the time and packaging changes, and every change would mean retraining the last layer and sending a new model out to every store.

So we map each image to an embedding, a vector \(\mathbf z = g(\text{image}) \in \mathbb R^D\), and the goal is that images of the same product end up close to each other. Recognising a product then becomes a search problem. With normalised embeddings, we measure closeness with cosine similarity,

$$\operatorname{sim}(\mathbf z, \mathbf z_j) = \frac{\mathbf z^\top \mathbf z_j}{\lVert\mathbf z\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf z_j\rVert},$$

and a FAISS vector index finds the most similar known products among the 10,000+ items. To add a new product, we add its embeddings to the index, and nothing has to be retrained.

Drag the two arrow tips. The similarity only depends on the angle between the vectors and not on their length. It is 1 when they point the same way, 0 at a right angle and −1 when they point in opposite directions.

Voting, and unknown products

Given the \(k\) nearest neighbours \(\mathcal N_k(\mathbf z)\), a simple rule is to let them vote:

$$\hat y = \arg\max_c \sum_{j \in \mathcal N_k(\mathbf z)} \mathbf 1[y_j = c].$$

A vote will always pick one of the known products, even when the item is something the system has never seen. In a store, a wrong answer at the checkout is worse than asking for help, so the system also needs to be able to answer that the product is unknown, for example when even the nearest neighbour is far away. This is often called the open-set problem, and it is why the demo at the bottom of this page has a threshold.

DINOv3

The embeddings come from DINOv3, which is a self-supervised vision transformer from Meta. It was trained on a very large number of images without labels, by learning to give similar representations to different views of the same image. The model therefore already has a good general sense of shape, texture and colour.

Being good in general is not the same as telling two almost identical yogurts apart under the lighting in a store, so the model had to be adapted to our data.

Fine-tuning with LoRA

Fine-tuning all the weights in a large model is expensive, needs a lot of data, and the model may forget some of what it already knew. Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) keeps the original weight matrix \(W_0 \in \mathbb R^{d\times k}\) frozen, and learns a low-rank update next to it:

$$\begin{gathered}W = W_0 + \Delta W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r}\,BA, \\ B \in \mathbb R^{d\times r},\; A \in \mathbb R^{r\times k},\; r \ll \min(d,k).\end{gathered}$$

\(B\) is initialised to zero, so training starts at the pre-trained model and only moves away from it as far as the data requires. The number of trained parameters is much smaller. For a single \(1024 \times 1024\) projection, which is a typical size in a large vision transformer, we get

$$\begin{gathered}\underbrace{1024^2 \approx 1.05\text{M}}_{\text{full fine-tuning}} \\ \text{vs.} \\ \underbrace{2 \cdot 8 \cdot 1024 \approx 16\text{k}}_{\text{LoRA, } r=8} \;\approx 1.6\%.\end{gathered}$$

Change the rank r. Even with r = 64, LoRA trains only a small part of the parameters that full fine-tuning would train for a 1024 × 1024 layer.

This makes training faster and needs less memory and data, and most of the general knowledge in the original model is kept.

Rare products and store lighting

Some products are bought so rarely that there are very few images of them, and the light at a checkout is very different from a photo studio. We therefore augmented the training data with flips, stretching and other geometric distortions, so that the model saw the rare products and the difficult lighting more often than it would otherwise. This improved the classification accuracy.

Some things I learned

Looking back, the most important decision was to treat recognition as a search problem. This is what made it possible to handle 10,000+ products and a catalogue that keeps changing. A foundation model with a small adapter also seems like a good default when there are few images per product.

The rare products were mostly a data problem, and they needed more varied training data more than a different model. Running on a small computer in the store also affects most of the other choices, since latency and memory are limited from the start.

Try it yourself

Real embeddings have hundreds of dimensions, but this one has two so that we can draw it. Move your pointer to place the unknown item. The three nearest products vote, and if even the closest one is outside the dashed circle, the item is marked as unknown.

Move the pointer around and lower the threshold. The three nearest products vote, and between the groups or far away from all of them, the item is marked as unknown.
