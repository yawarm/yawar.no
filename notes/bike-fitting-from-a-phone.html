Bike fitting from a phone camera

A professional bike fit means a studio and an expert. At APIIR we put it in an app: pose estimation, a bit of biomechanics, and 20,000+ riders.

2022–25 · 7 min read

A good bike fit makes riding faster, more comfortable and less likely to hurt your knees. The traditional way to get one is a studio visit: an expert, a trainer, sometimes motion-capture markers on your joints, and a bill to match. At APIIR we asked whether a phone could do most of that job.

From 2022 to 2025 I was CPO, full-stack developer and lead data scientist there. We took the app from zero to 20,000+ active users on iOS and Android, with a 4.5★ rating on the App Store, and raised more than $300K along the way. This note is about the technical core: turning a video of you on your bike into concrete adjustments.

The pipeline

At a high level, the app does three things:

  1. See the rider. Pose estimation finds the body's joints in every frame of the video.
  2. Measure the rider. From the joints we compute angles and how they change through the pedal stroke.
  3. Advise the rider. A biomechanical model turns those measurements, together with the rider's body and bike, into personalised adjustments with centimetre-level precision.

Pose estimation

A pose-estimation model takes an image and outputs keypoints: pixel coordinates \((u_k, v_k)\) for joints like shoulder, hip, knee, ankle, elbow and wrist, each with a confidence score \(c_k \in [0, 1]\). Modern models do this in real time on a phone, but a single frame is never perfect. Joints get hidden behind the bike frame, clothes blur outlines, and the camera is rarely exactly side-on.

The saving grace is that pedalling is periodic. You don't need one perfect frame; you get many pedal strokes, and can combine them. A simple and robust idea is to weight each measurement by its confidence and to average over the same phase of many strokes:

$$\hat\theta(\phi) = \frac{\sum_{s} c_{s}(\phi)\,\theta_{s}(\phi)}{\sum_{s} c_{s}(\phi)},$$

where \(\phi\) is the crank angle (the phase of the pedal stroke) and \(s\) runs over strokes. Noise that's random from stroke to stroke averages out; the rider's real posture doesn't.

Try: raise the noise, then add more pedal strokes. Notice: a single stroke (grey dots) is all over the place, but the average over many strokes (orange) hugs the true knee angle (dashed).

From keypoints to angles

Most of bike fitting is about a handful of joint angles. Given three keypoints, say hip \(\mathbf h\), knee \(\mathbf k\) and ankle \(\mathbf a\), the knee angle is the angle between the thigh and the shin:

$$\theta_{\text{knee}} = \arccos\!\left(\frac{(\mathbf h - \mathbf k)\cdot(\mathbf a - \mathbf k)}{\lVert\mathbf h - \mathbf k\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf a - \mathbf k\rVert}\right).$$

The same formula with different joints gives the hip angle (shoulder, hip, knee), the elbow angle and the back angle. Tracked over a whole pedal stroke, each one becomes a curve. The most informative points on those curves are the extremes, especially when the pedal is at the bottom of the stroke and the leg is closest to straight.

Try: drag the hip, knee and ankle. Notice: three keypoints are all it takes to measure a joint angle, and moving the camera closer (scaling all points) doesn't change it.

Angles also have a nice property for a phone app: they don't depend on how far away the camera is. Distances in pixels do, so turning "raise the saddle" into "raise the saddle by 1.5 cm" needs a scale, for example from a known length on the bike or the rider's own measurements.

From angles to adjustments

Bike fitting has a long list of rules of thumb. One classic is to set the saddle height to about 0.883 × inseam, measured from the centre of the bottom bracket. Another is that the knee should still be slightly bent at the bottom of the stroke, typically somewhere around 25–40° of flexion, depending on the rider and the style of riding.

Rules like these are a starting point, but they ignore the actual rider: flexibility, how they sit, how they pedal. That's where measuring beats formulas. Our fitting algorithms fused the pose estimates with a biomechanical model of the rider and the bike, so a recommendation like "saddle up" came from the measured angles, the rider's body and the bike's geometry together.

The underlying geometry is simple. Treat the leg as two rigid segments, thigh length \(L_t\) and shin length \(L_s\), between the hip and the pedal. If the distance from hip to pedal is \(d\), the law of cosines gives the knee angle directly:

$$d^2 = L_t^2 + L_s^2 - 2L_tL_s\cos\theta_{\text{knee}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_{\text{knee}} = \arccos\!\left(\frac{L_t^2 + L_s^2 - d^2}{2L_tL_s}\right).$$

Raise the saddle and \(d\) grows at the bottom of the stroke, so the knee opens up. That's the relationship the demo at the bottom of this page lets you play with.

Building it as a product

The model was only part of the job. As CPO I also owned the roadmap, user research and go-to-market, and I architected the platform: a Flutter app for both iOS and Android from one codebase, a Django backend, and cloud infrastructure built to scale from day one.

  • The video is part of the problem. A pose model can only work with what it's given, so camera angle and distance matter as much as the model.
  • Precision you can act on. Advice is only useful if a rider can actually make the change with an Allen key, which is why centimetre-level precision was the target.

Try it yourself

A simple 2D rider: the leg is two rigid segments from the hip (on the saddle) to the pedal. Change the saddle height and watch the knee angle through the pedal stroke. The shaded band is the typical range of knee flexion at the bottom of the stroke.

Try: drag the saddle height. Notice: the marker shows knee flexion at the bottom of the stroke. Too low and the knee stays bent; too high and the leg straightens.

A deliberately simple model: fixed thigh and shin lengths, the hip fixed on the saddle, the foot on the pedal circle. Real fitting also has to deal with ankle movement, saddle setback and how the rider actually sits.
