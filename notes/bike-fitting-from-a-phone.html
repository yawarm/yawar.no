Can a phone camera fit your bike?

At APIIR we made an app that gives a bike fit from a phone video, using pose estimation and a biomechanical model. It got more than 20,000 users.

2022–25 · 7 min read

A good bike fit makes riding more comfortable and reduces the risk of knee pain and other injuries. The usual way to get one is to visit a studio, where an expert adjusts the bike, sometimes with motion capture markers on your joints, and this is quite expensive. At APIIR we wanted to see how much of this could be done with a phone.

From 2022 to 2025 I was CPO, full-stack developer and lead data scientist at APIIR. We built the app from zero to more than 20,000 active users on iOS and Android, with a 4.5★ rating on the App Store, and raised more than $300K. In this note I write about the technical part, which is how a video of you on your bike becomes concrete adjustments.

The pipeline

The app does three things:

  1. Pose estimation finds the joints of the rider in each frame of the video.
  2. From the joints we compute angles, and how they change through the pedal stroke.
  3. A biomechanical model combines the measurements with the body of the rider and the bike, and gives personalised adjustments with centimetre level precision.

Pose estimation

A pose estimation model takes an image and returns keypoints, which are pixel coordinates \((u_k, v_k)\) for joints like the shoulder, hip, knee, ankle, elbow and wrist, each with a confidence \(c_k \in [0, 1]\). Modern models can do this in real time on a phone, but a single frame is rarely perfect. Joints can be hidden behind the frame of the bike, clothes make the outline unclear, and the camera is almost never exactly from the side.

Pedalling is periodic, which helps. We do not need one perfect frame, since we get many pedal strokes that we can combine. A simple way to do this is to weight each measurement by its confidence, and average over the same phase of many strokes:

$$\hat\theta(\phi) = \frac{\sum_{s} c_{s}(\phi)\,\theta_{s}(\phi)}{\sum_{s} c_{s}(\phi)},$$

where \(\phi\) is the crank angle, meaning the phase of the pedal stroke, and \(s\) goes over the strokes. Noise that is random from stroke to stroke averages out, while the actual posture of the rider stays the same.

Increase the noise and then the number of pedal strokes. A single stroke (grey dots) is very noisy, but the average over many strokes (orange) is close to the true knee angle (dashed).

From keypoints to angles

Most of bike fitting is about a few joint angles. Given three keypoints, for example the hip \(\mathbf h\), the knee \(\mathbf k\) and the ankle \(\mathbf a\), the knee angle is the angle between the thigh and the shin:

$$\theta_{\text{knee}} = \arccos\!\left(\frac{(\mathbf h - \mathbf k)\cdot(\mathbf a - \mathbf k)}{\lVert\mathbf h - \mathbf k\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf a - \mathbf k\rVert}\right).$$

With other joints, the same formula gives the hip angle (shoulder, hip and knee), the elbow angle and the back angle. If we track an angle over a whole pedal stroke, we get a curve, and the most useful points on it are the extremes, especially when the pedal is at the bottom and the leg is closest to straight.

Drag the hip, the knee and the ankle. Three keypoints are enough to compute the angle, and moving the camera closer (scaling all the points) does not change it.

Angles also do not depend on how far away the camera is, while distances in pixels do. To turn "raise the saddle" into "raise the saddle by 1.5 cm", we therefore need a scale, for example from a known length on the bike or from the measurements of the rider.

From angles to adjustments

There are many rules of thumb in bike fitting. A classic one is to set the saddle height to about 0.883 times the inseam, measured from the centre of the bottom bracket. Another is that the knee should still be slightly bent at the bottom of the stroke, typically around 25–40° of flexion, depending on the rider and the type of riding.

These rules are a starting point, but they do not take into account how flexible the rider is, how they sit or how they pedal. Our fitting algorithms combined the pose estimates with a biomechanical model of the rider and the bike, so a recommendation like raising the saddle came from the measured angles, the body of the rider and the geometry of the bike together.

The geometry behind this is simple. If we treat the leg as two rigid segments, the thigh with length \(L_t\) and the shin with length \(L_s\), and the distance from the hip to the pedal is \(d\), the law of cosines gives the knee angle directly:

$$\begin{gathered}d^2 = L_t^2 + L_s^2 - 2L_tL_s\cos\theta_{\text{knee}} \\ \Longrightarrow\; \theta_{\text{knee}} = \arccos\!\left(\frac{L_t^2 + L_s^2 - d^2}{2L_tL_s}\right).\end{gathered}$$

When the saddle is raised, \(d\) increases at the bottom of the stroke, and the knee opens up. This is the relationship in the demo at the bottom of the page.

Building the product

The model was only one part of the work. As CPO I was also responsible for the roadmap, user research and go-to-market, and I designed the platform, with a Flutter app for both iOS and Android from one codebase, a Django backend, and cloud infrastructure built to scale.

A pose model can only work with the video it gets, so how the phone is placed and the angle of the camera matter as much as the model. The advice also has to be precise enough that a rider can make the change with an Allen key, which is why we aimed for centimetre level precision.

Try it yourself

This is a simple rider in 2D, where the leg is two rigid segments from the hip on the saddle to the pedal. You can change the saddle height and see how the knee angle changes through the pedal stroke. The shaded band is the typical range of knee flexion at the bottom of the stroke.

When you change the saddle height, the marker shows the knee flexion at the bottom of the stroke. With a low saddle the knee stays bent, and with a high saddle the leg becomes straight.

This is a simple model with fixed thigh and shin lengths, the hip fixed on the saddle and the foot on the pedal circle. A real bike fit also has to take into account ankle movement, saddle setback and how the rider sits.
