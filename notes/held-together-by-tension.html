Held together by tension

Tensegrity structures look like they shouldn't stand. Finding their shape is an energy minimisation problem.

2024 · 4 min read

Tensegrity structures are those sculptures where rigid bars seem to float in mid-air, held in place only by cables. No bar touches another. Finding the shape they settle into is an optimisation problem, and it has more structure than I expected.

A structure as a graph

A tensegrity is a graph \(G = (V, E)\): joints are vertices, and every edge is either a bar or a cable. If joint \(i\) sits at \(x^{(i)} \in \mathbb R^3\), the unknown is the vector of all positions \(X = (x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}) \in \mathbb R^{3N}\). The structure settles where its total energy is as low as possible.

The energy

Bars are stiff springs that resist both stretching and squashing. With rest length \(\ell_{ij}\) and current length \(L_{ij} = \lVert x^{(i)} - x^{(j)}\rVert\):

$$E^{\text{bar}}_{\text{elast}}(e_{ij}) = \frac{c}{2\ell_{ij}^2}\,(L_{ij} - \ell_{ij})^2.$$

Gravity pulls on the bars' mass, with line density \(\rho\):

$$E^{\text{bar}}_{\text{grav}}(e_{ij}) = \rho g\,\ell_{ij}\,\frac{x_3^{(i)} + x_3^{(j)}}{2}.$$

Cables only resist stretching. A slack cable does nothing:

$$E^{\text{cable}}_{\text{elast}}(e_{ij}) = \begin{cases}\dfrac{k}{2\ell_{ij}^2}\,(L_{ij} - \ell_{ij})^2 & L_{ij} \gt \ell_{ij},\\[4pt] 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

And external loads, weights \(m_i\) hanging from the joints: \(E_{\text{ext}} = \sum_i m_i g\, x_3^{(i)}\). The objective is the sum:

$$E(X) = \sum_{e_{ij} \in \mathcal B}\big(E^{\text{bar}}_{\text{elast}} + E^{\text{bar}}_{\text{grav}}\big) + \sum_{e_{ij} \in \mathcal C} E^{\text{cable}}_{\text{elast}} + E_{\text{ext}}(X).$$

Two kinds of constraints keep it physical: some joints are fixed, \(x^{(i)} = p^{(i)}\), and nothing may go below the ground, \(x^{(i)}_3 \ge f(x^{(i)}_1, x^{(i)}_2)\).

Existence of solutions

With fixed joints and a connected graph, every free joint is within a bounded distance of a fixed one, so the feasible set is compact. \(E\) is continuous, and the extreme value theorem guarantees a global minimum. With a ground profile instead, you need the ground to rise towards infinity away from the origin (coercivity). On a perfectly flat floor the argument breaks down: mathematically, a structure could keep spreading out and lowering its energy.

Cable nets

With only cables and fixed joints, the problem is well behaved.

  • It's \(C^1\), but not \(C^2\). The gradient of a cable term, \(\frac{k}{\ell_{ij}^2}\big(x^{(i)} - x^{(j)}\big)\big(1 - \frac{\ell_{ij}}{L_{ij}}\big)\), goes smoothly to zero as the cable goes slack. The second derivative jumps there.
  • It's convex, but not strictly. Each cable term is a convex function of a norm, and the load term is linear.

So \(\nabla E(X^*) = 0\) is not just necessary but sufficient: any stationary point is a global minimum. With 4 fixed corners, 4 free joints, 8 cables of length 3, \(k = 3\) and \(mg = 1/6\), BFGS found the exact solution from a naive starting guess:

Adding bars

Bars break convexity. A quick counterexample: take \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(y = -x\). Then \(E\big(\tfrac12 x + \tfrac12 y\big) = E(0)\), where every bar is collapsed to zero length and stores energy \(\sum k/2 \gt 0\), while the average \(\tfrac12E(x) + \tfrac12E(-x)\) can be zero. The convexity inequality fails.

Try: click anywhere on the curve to drop a ball, then switch between the two energies. Notice: with cables only, every ball rolls to the same bottom (convex). Add a bar and the landscape gets two valleys, so where you start decides where you end up.

So now \(\nabla E = 0\) is only necessary, and different starting points can end in different local minima. Each of those is still a physically stable state, just maybe not the lowest one. For a tensegrity dome (4 fixed joints, 4 bars of length 10, 8 cables):

BFGS

BFGS is a quasi-Newton method. Instead of computing the Hessian, it builds up an approximation of the inverse Hessian \(H_k\) from gradients only. With step \(s_k = X_{k+1} - X_k\) and gradient change \(y_k = \nabla E_{k+1} - \nabla E_k\):

$$H_{k+1} = \big(I - \rho_k s_k y_k^\top\big)H_k\big(I - \rho_k y_k s_k^\top\big) + \rho_k s_k s_k^\top, \qquad \rho_k = \frac{1}{y_k^\top s_k}.$$

Each step size is chosen by a line search satisfying the Wolfe conditions. The Armijo condition makes sure each step actually lowers the energy enough; the curvature condition prevents steps that are too short to make progress:

$$E(X_k + \alpha p_k) \le E(X_k) + c_1\alpha\,\nabla E_k^\top p_k, \qquad \nabla E(X_k + \alpha p_k)^\top p_k \ge c_2\,\nabla E_k^\top p_k.$$

We used \(c_1 = 0.1\) and an extrapolation factor of 3, tuned by trial and error.

Free-standing structures

Finally, I removed the fixed joints, added cables along the base, and let the structure stand on a bowl-shaped ground \(f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1^2 + x_2^2)/20\). The ground constraint was enforced with a penalty term (scale 0.1), so the optimiser trades energy against constraint violation. From a rough initial guess, it found a balanced dome in which every cable is taut:

Try it yourself

The cable problem from above, in 2D and alive: a cable made of short segments, hanging between two anchors. Just like in the real problem, segments only pull (a slack one does nothing), gravity pulls on the joints, and the whole thing slides downhill in energy until it settles.

Try: click a joint to hang a weight on it, drag a joint and let go, or drag one of the black anchors. Notice: the cable always settles into the shape with the lowest energy. Weights make the segments above them pull harder (redder); push the anchors together and the cable goes slack (dashed), because a cable can’t push.

Dashed lines are slack cables. Colour shows how hard a cable is being stretched.
