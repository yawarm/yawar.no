How does a tensegrity structure find its shape?

Tensegrity structures are held up by cables alone. We found their shape by minimising the energy with BFGS.

2024 · 4 min read

Tensegrity structures are structures where rigid bars are held in place only by cables, and no two bars touch each other. They look like they should not be able to stand. Finding the shape they settle into can be written as an optimisation problem, which is what this project was about.

A structure as a graph

We represent a tensegrity structure as a graph \(G = (V, E)\), where the joints are vertices and each edge is either a bar or a cable. If joint \(i\) is at \(x^{(i)} \in \mathbb R^3\), the unknown is the vector of all positions \(X = (x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}) \in \mathbb R^{3N}\). The structure settles where its total energy is as low as possible.

The energy

Bars are stiff springs, which resist being both stretched and compressed. With rest length \(\ell_{ij}\) and current length \(L_{ij} = \lVert x^{(i)} - x^{(j)}\rVert\), the elastic energy of a bar is

$$E^{\text{bar}}_{\text{elast}}(e_{ij}) = \frac{c}{2\ell_{ij}^2}\,(L_{ij} - \ell_{ij})^2.$$

The bars also have mass, and with line density \(\rho\) the gravitational energy is

$$E^{\text{bar}}_{\text{grav}}(e_{ij}) = \rho g\,\ell_{ij}\,\frac{x_3^{(i)} + x_3^{(j)}}{2}.$$

Cables only resist being stretched, and a slack cable has no energy:

$$E^{\text{cable}}_{\text{elast}}(e_{ij}) = \begin{cases}\dfrac{k}{2\ell_{ij}^2}\,(L_{ij} - \ell_{ij})^2 & L_{ij} \gt \ell_{ij},\\[4pt] 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

Finally, there can be external loads, which are weights \(m_i\) hanging from the joints, with energy \(E_{\text{ext}} = \sum_i m_i g\, x_3^{(i)}\). The objective function is the sum of these terms:

$$\begin{aligned}E(X) = {} & \sum_{e_{ij} \in \mathcal B}\big(E^{\text{bar}}_{\text{elast}} + E^{\text{bar}}_{\text{grav}}\big) \\ & + \sum_{e_{ij} \in \mathcal C} E^{\text{cable}}_{\text{elast}} + E_{\text{ext}}(X).\end{aligned}$$

We also have two kinds of constraints. Some joints can be fixed, \(x^{(i)} = p^{(i)}\), and no joint can go below the ground, \(x^{(i)}_3 \ge f(x^{(i)}_1, x^{(i)}_2)\).

Existence of solutions

With fixed joints and a connected graph, every free joint is within a bounded distance of a fixed joint, so the feasible set is compact. \(E\) is continuous, and by the extreme value theorem it has a global minimum. With a ground constraint, we need the ground to rise towards infinity away from the origin (coercivity). On a completely flat floor this argument does not work, since the structure could in principle keep spreading out and lower its energy.

Cable nets

If we only have cables and fixed joints, the problem is well behaved. The objective is \(C^1\) but not \(C^2\). The gradient of a cable term, \(\frac{k}{\ell_{ij}^2}\big(x^{(i)} - x^{(j)}\big)\big(1 - \frac{\ell_{ij}}{L_{ij}}\big)\), goes to zero when the cable goes slack, but the second derivative jumps at that point. The objective is also convex, but not strictly convex, since each cable term is a convex function of a norm and the load term is linear.

This means that \(\nabla E(X^*) = 0\) is both necessary and sufficient, so any stationary point is a global minimum. With 4 fixed corners, 4 free joints, 8 cables of length 3, \(k = 3\) and \(mg = 1/6\), BFGS found the exact solution from a simple starting guess:

Adding bars

When we add bars, the problem is no longer convex. To see this, take \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(y = -x\). Then \(E\big(\tfrac12 x + \tfrac12 y\big) = E(0)\), where every bar has length zero and the energy is \(\sum k/2 \gt 0\), while the average \(\tfrac12E(x) + \tfrac12E(-x)\) can be zero. So the convexity condition does not hold.

Click on the curve to drop a ball, and switch between the two energies. With only cables, every ball rolls to the same minimum. With a bar the curve has two minima, so the end point depends on where the ball starts.

This means that \(\nabla E = 0\) is only a necessary condition, and different starting points can end up in different local minima. Each local minimum still corresponds to a stable physical state, but not necessarily the one with the lowest energy. For a tensegrity dome with 4 fixed joints, 4 bars of length 10 and 8 cables, we got:

BFGS

BFGS is a quasi-Newton method, which builds up an approximation \(H_k\) of the inverse Hessian from the gradients, without computing the Hessian itself. With the step \(s_k = X_{k+1} - X_k\) and the change in gradient \(y_k = \nabla E_{k+1} - \nabla E_k\), the update is

$$\begin{aligned}H_{k+1} &= \big(I - \rho_k s_k y_k^\top\big)H_k\big(I - \rho_k y_k s_k^\top\big) \\ &\quad + \rho_k s_k s_k^\top, \\ \rho_k &= \frac{1}{y_k^\top s_k}.\end{aligned}$$

The step length is found with a line search that satisfies the Wolfe conditions. The Armijo condition makes sure that each step lowers the energy enough, and the curvature condition stops the steps from being too short:

$$\begin{gathered}E(X_k + \alpha p_k) \le E(X_k) + c_1\alpha\,\nabla E_k^\top p_k, \\ \nabla E(X_k + \alpha p_k)^\top p_k \ge c_2\,\nabla E_k^\top p_k.\end{gathered}$$

We used \(c_1 = 0.1\) and an extrapolation factor of 3, which we found by trial and error.

Free-standing structures

In the last part I removed the fixed joints, added cables along the base and let the structure stand on a bowl shaped ground \(f(x_1, x_2) = (x_1^2 + x_2^2)/20\). The ground constraint was added as a penalty term with scale 0.1, so the optimiser trades off energy against breaking the constraint. From a rough starting guess, it found a stable dome where all the cables are tight:

The top joints ended up at a height of about 9.17, which is close to the 9.54 of the dome with fixed corners. We would not expect them to be equal, since the base now rests on a curved floor and is not fixed at height zero.

Try it yourself

This is the cable problem from above in 2D, with a cable made of short segments hanging between two anchors. As in the full problem, the segments can only pull, gravity pulls on the joints, and the cable moves towards lower energy until it settles.

Click a joint to hang a weight on it, or drag a joint or one of the black anchors. The cable settles in the shape with the lowest energy. The segments above a weight pull harder and turn red, and if the anchors are moved close together the cable goes slack (dashed), since a cable can only pull.

Dashed lines are slack cables. Colour shows how hard a cable is being stretched.
