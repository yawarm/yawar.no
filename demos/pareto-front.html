Can concrete be both cheap and green?

A small genetic algorithm searches for concrete recipes that are both cheap and low in CO₂. You can't have both, so it finds the trade-off curve.

In the concrete project there was not one best recipe. Cheaper mixes with more cement emit more CO₂, while replacing cement with other binders lowers the CO₂ but costs more, and every recipe still has to satisfy the regulations. This is a multi-objective problem, which I solved with NSGA-II on top of regression tree models of how a recipe behaves.

Several objectives

We want to minimise several objectives at once, here cost and CO₂, \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = \big(f_1(\mathbf x), f_2(\mathbf x)\big)\), under constraints \(g(\mathbf x) \le 0\). A recipe \(\mathbf a\) dominates \(\mathbf b\) if it is at least as good in every objective, and strictly better in at least one:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbf a \prec \mathbf b \iff {} & f_m(\mathbf a) \le f_m(\mathbf b)\;\;\forall m \\ & \text{and}\;\; f_m(\mathbf a) \lt f_m(\mathbf b)\;\;\text{for some } m.\end{aligned}$$

The recipes that are not dominated by any other recipe form the Pareto front. Each point on the front is a different trade-off, where the CO₂ cannot be lowered without a higher cost, and choosing between them is a decision for people and not for the algorithm.

When you move over the points, the orange area contains the recipes that the chosen one beats, since they are both more expensive and have more CO₂. Points that no other point beats form the Pareto front.

NSGA-II

NSGA-II is based on three ideas:

  1. The population is sorted into fronts by non-dominated sorting, where front 1 is the non-dominated set, front 2 is the non-dominated set after front 1 is removed, and so on.
  2. Within a front, points in less crowded regions are preferred, measured by the crowding distance, so the front stays spread out. For point \(i\) in a front sorted by each objective \(m\): $$d_i = \sum_m \frac{f_m^{(i+1)} - f_m^{(i-1)}}{f_m^{\max} - f_m^{\min}},$$ and the extreme points get \(d = \infty\), so they are always kept.
  3. Parents and children compete together (elitism). The next generation is filled front by front, and ties in the last front are broken by crowding distance.

Constraints are handled with constraint domination, which means that a feasible recipe always beats an infeasible one, and between two infeasible recipes the one with the smallest violation wins.

Try it yourself

This simplified version evolves a population of recipes, each with a cement amount and a replacement share. The orange points are the Pareto front. If you raise the strength requirement, the front moves. The coefficients are made up, but the trade-off is the same.

Press run and then increase the strength requirement. The orange points form a curve where a cheaper recipe always means more CO₂, and a stricter requirement moves the whole curve up and to the right.

In the actual project

In the project, the objectives came from regression trees fitted to data, which made each evaluation fast and possible to explain. The regulations decided which recipes were allowed at all, and constraint domination kept the search inside them. The result was a set of possible recipes, which turns the question of which recipe is best into a question of how much one is willing to pay for each kilo of CO₂ saved.
