Evolve a greener recipe

A small genetic algorithm searches for concrete recipes that are both cheap and low in CO₂. You can't have both, so it finds the trade-off curve.

The concrete project didn't have one "best" recipe. Cheaper mixes with more cement emit more CO₂; replacing cement with other binders cuts CO₂ but costs more. And every recipe still has to satisfy the regulations. That's a multi-objective problem, and I solved it with NSGA-II on top of regression tree models of how a recipe behaves.

Several objectives

We want to minimise several objectives at once, here cost and CO₂, \(\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = \big(f_1(\mathbf x), f_2(\mathbf x)\big)\), subject to constraints \(g(\mathbf x) \le 0\). Recipe \(\mathbf a\) dominates \(\mathbf b\) if it's at least as good on every objective and strictly better on at least one:

$$\mathbf a \prec \mathbf b \iff f_m(\mathbf a) \le f_m(\mathbf b)\;\;\forall m \;\;\text{and}\;\; f_m(\mathbf a) \lt f_m(\mathbf b)\;\;\text{for some } m.$$

The recipes nobody dominates form the Pareto front. Each point on it is a different, defensible trade-off: you can't lower CO₂ without paying more. Choosing among them is a decision for people, not for the algorithm.

Try: move over the points. Notice: the orange box is everything the chosen recipe beats (more expensive and more CO₂). Points no box can reach form the Pareto front.

NSGA-II

  1. Non-dominated sorting. Rank the population into fronts: front 1 is the non-dominated set, front 2 is what's non-dominated once front 1 is removed, and so on.
  2. Crowding distance. Within a front, prefer points in empty regions, so the front stays spread out instead of piling up in one spot. For point \(i\) in a front sorted by each objective \(m\): $$d_i = \sum_m \frac{f_m^{(i+1)} - f_m^{(i-1)}}{f_m^{\max} - f_m^{\min}},$$ with the extreme points given \(d = \infty\) so they're always kept.
  3. Elitism. Parents and children compete together. Fill the next generation front by front, and break ties in the last front by crowding distance.

Constraints are handled by constraint domination: a feasible recipe always beats an infeasible one, and between two infeasible ones, the smaller violation wins.

Try it yourself

This toy version evolves a population of recipes (cement amount and replacement share). The orange points are the Pareto front. Raise the strength requirement and watch the front move. The coefficients are made up; the trade-off isn't.

Try: press run, then drag the strength requirement up. Notice: the orange points spread out along a curve: you can't get cheaper without more CO₂. Stricter rules push the whole front up and to the right.

The real version

  • The objectives came from models. Instead of formulas, regression trees fitted to data predicted each recipe's properties, which made every evaluation fast and explainable.
  • The constraints came from the rules. Regulatory requirements defined which recipes were allowed at all, and constraint domination kept the search inside them.
  • The output was a menu, not an answer. The value of the Pareto front is that it turns "what's the best recipe?" into "how much are you willing to pay per kilo of CO₂ saved?"
